We live in a world where almost every productivity problem gets the same proposed solution: make it an app. Track it digitally. Sync it. Add notifications. Put it in the cloud.

And yet some of the most reliable systems in my life are made of paper cards, folded A4 sheets, and tiny physical markers placed exactly where I will bump into them.

The reason is simple: **physical cues do not need to win a battle for attention inside a screen that is already crowded with everything else**.

<!--more-->

I learned this lesson the hard way through a few very ordinary household problems.

## The habit tracker that finally worked

I have tried many digital habit trackers over the years. They usually work for about a week. Then I forget to open them, the streak breaks, and the whole thing quietly disappears from my life.

The only habit tracker that has consistently worked for me is a piece of A4 paper taped to the door of my living room.

I fold it into a grid so each row represents one week. Every time I walk past that door, I see it. I do not need to remember to open an app. I do not need a notification. The reminder is already waiting for me in the physical world.

That visibility matters more than all the features in the digital trackers I tried. The paper is not smarter. It is just harder to ignore.

This is exactly what Don Norman describes in _The Design of Everyday Things_ as **"knowledge in the world"**. Instead of forcing yourself to remember something internally, you place the knowledge into the environment where it can do part of the remembering for you.

Norman's point is that information placed in the world can act as a reminder in its own right, while memory stored only in our heads is fragile because we still have to retrieve it at the right moment.

He gives the example of putting a book on the floor in front of the front door so you cannot leave home without noticing it. My A4 habit tracker is basically the same trick, just adapted to modern life.

## The coffee machine card

Another recurring problem in my home is cleaning the brewing unit of our fully automatic coffee machine.

The trigger should be simple: when the coffee grounds container is full, it is usually also a good time to wash the brewing unit.

But in practice, the brewing unit can only be removed when the machine is turned off. So what happens is this:

1. I see the "empty grounds container" signal.
2. I clear the grounds container.
3. I make a coffee.
4. I run to a virtual meeting, thinking that I will come back and wash the brewing unit after the meeting finishes.
5. I completely forget to wash the brewing unit.

So I made a tiny physical card. One side is blank. The other side says: **Wash brewing unit**.

When I empty the grounds container, I flip the card. Then the next time I walk up to the machine, I see the flipped card before I turn it on. Sometimes I notice it while cleaning the kitchen and wash the unit then. Either way, the system works far better than trying to remember it mentally.

What makes this work is another Norman idea: a reminder needs both a **signal** and a **message**.

In Norman's framing, a reminder works only when something catches your attention and also tells you what action is needed.

A note hidden somewhere has the message but no signal. A random object tied to your finger has the signal but not always the message. The flipped card does both.

## The air fryer card

I use the same idea for the air fryer.

After cooking, the basket is too hot to put directly into the dishwasher, and also too hot to leave casually on the countertop. So it often stays inside the air fryer to cool down. And once it disappears back into the machine, it becomes easy to forget that it still needs washing.

My solution is a small card that says **Wash air fryer**. I cut it with a hook like those hotel "do not disturb" signs and hang it on the dishwasher handle whenever I have used the air fryer.

Now the reminder appears in the exact place where the next action should happen.

This is such a low-tech solution that it almost feels silly to describe. But that is exactly why I like it. It accepts how human attention actually works instead of pretending I will suddenly become better at remembering invisible tasks.

## A travel booklet beats app-hopping

I recently started using another paper-based system when traveling.

I fold a sheet of A4 paper into a tiny A7-size booklet, [using this folding method](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UqsSKJzKC00). On it I write the core information for the trip:

- flight time
- airport code
- flight number
- booking reference
- how to get from the airport to the hotel
- which bus or train to take
- which direction to go
- where to get on and off

Yes, all of this already exists on my phone.

But a busy airport is one of the worst places to keep unlocking a phone, switching between apps, balancing luggage, and scrolling for the right detail. It is stressful, slow, and it makes me feel like an easy target for pickpockets.

The little paper booklet works as a fast external memory. I can glance at it instantly. No battery. No unlocking. No searching.

There is another benefit too: the act of writing the information down forces me to mentally rehearse the trip.

While copying the details by hand, I often notice something I had not thought through before. Maybe I need to get to the airport during rush hour. Maybe I should schedule a taxi. Maybe I do not actually know which train direction to take after landing.

So even though writing it feels inefficient on paper, it is actually a useful reflection step. It reminds me of the "migration" idea from bullet journaling, where you rewrite tasks or plans into a new context and, in doing so, decide whether they still matter and whether the plan still makes sense.

And because the booklet is dirt cheap, losing it does not hurt much. I also keep it in my pocket, and I have never had any trouble taking it through airport security.

## When the information changes often, make the cue physical and the data digital

So far, all of my examples have been fully physical. But the same principle also works when the information itself needs to stay digital and easy to update.

Of course, not every checklist should be permanently printed on paper.

Sometimes the information changes quickly, so what you really want is a hybrid: **a physical trigger that opens digital content**.

For example, the checklist of things I need when leaving home with my child keeps changing. One month, diapers are essential; a little later, they are not.

A paper checklist would need to be replaced or crossed out too often. So instead, I use a round black NFC sticker on the door frame. These stickers are very cheap to buy online, and you can easily use an open-source Android app to write a URL or deep link onto one. My tag points to an Obsidian note containing the current checklist.

When I am leaving home, I scan the tag with my phone and, in my setup, the checklist opens right away.

The important design choice is not the NFC technology itself. It is the fact that the reminder has been made physical. I do not have to remember that a checklist exists. The tag is sitting there in the real world, where departure actually happens.

I use a similar idea for luggage tags. After someone abused my email address to sign up for AI free trials, I became much more careful about exposing personal contact details.

So my luggage tag now has only my name and a QR code. The QR code points to a web page with contact information that I can update per trip. I can change what is visible, remove details I do not want to expose, or add temporary contact information when needed. The page only needs minimal contact details, not a full personal profile or home address.

That is the kind of digital flexibility I genuinely like: the data can change quickly, but the trigger remains a simple physical object.

## AI might make physical systems more practical, not less

The same pattern shows up again here: a physical-first workflow becomes much more useful when digital tools can support it afterward.

One reason people default to digital tools is that physical notes used to create friction later. They were hard to search, hard to share, and annoying to convert back into digital form.

That is becoming less true.

I have considered buying an e-ink writing tablet like a reMarkable for a long time because its value proposition is exactly this: write by hand, then convert handwriting into digital text, while also making handwritten notes easier to search and organize.

But in my own experiments, I noticed that mainstream multimodal tools like Gemini, ChatGPT, and Claude could read my ugly cursive notes with surprisingly high accuracy. That changes the equation.

It also means you no longer need specialized notebooks with markers on the paper plus a vendor-specific scanning app just to get notes back into digital form. In my own tests, a casual photo of one of my handwritten notes was often enough for those tools to digitize it reliably.

It means you do not necessarily have to choose between physical and digital upfront. You can think on paper first, then let AI help digitize it later.

For this post, that is exactly the workflow I used: pocket notebook notes first, AI-assisted extraction later, then expansion into a draft.

In other words, AI may actually strengthen the case for physical tools. If digitizing handwritten notes becomes cheap and reliable, then paper regains many of the advantages that software had over it.

## The real lesson

I do not think the lesson here is "paper is better than software." Digital tools are still essential for calendars, shared documents, communication, and anything that changes rapidly or needs to sync across devices.

The real lesson is that **we should design reminders and workflows based on where attention actually lives**.

If a task depends on a future version of me remembering something at the right moment, then placing a cue into the physical environment is often more reliable than placing it into a phone full of notifications.

And if the information behind the cue needs to stay flexible, we can attach the physical world to digital content using NFC tags, QR codes, or AI-powered digitization.

In a screen-first world, tactile cues are not obsolete. They are often the missing layer that makes a system actually work.
